\begin{abstract}
Software testing education often emphasizes confirming expected behavior, leaving students less prepared to uncover unexpected failures. We present a classroom workshop using grammar-based fuzz testing with Fandango to promote exploratory, robustness-oriented testing among master's students. Its four phases progressed from concept and live demonstration to guided replication and open-ended fuzzing of students' own faults or programs. We evaluated it as a pilot with 22 students, using pre/post self-assessments (20 matched pairs), a multiple-choice knowledge item, and perception data. Self-reported familiarity with grammar-based fuzzing rose by 2.05 points on a 5-point scale and understanding of random versus grammar-based fuzzing by 2.10, both large, statistically significant changes. The knowledge item, answered correctly by 18 of 20 students beforehand, did not improve. Students rated usability and engagement positively; installation was the main difficulty. All materials are public; with self-reports from one cohort and no control group, the results are exploratory.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
software testing education, grammar-based fuzzing, robustness-oriented testing, active learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

A fundamental limitation of traditional software testing education is its emphasis on \emph{correctness validation}: students learn to write test cases that confirm expected outputs for expected inputs~\cite{garousi2020testing}. Although necessary, this approach may leave students unprepared to identify the failures that matter most in practice, such as those caused by unexpected inputs, edge cases, malformed data, and adversarial conditions that developers may not initially anticipate.

Consider a simple scenario: a developer writes a command-line calculator and verifies it works for \texttt{ADD 2 3} and \texttt{MUL 4 5}.
All tests pass.
But what happens when the input is \texttt{DIV 7 0}?
Or \texttt{ADD 999999 999999}?
Or simply an empty string?
Traditional testing rarely finds these failures because it tests what developers \emph{expect}, not what programs \emph{cannot handle}.

Fuzz testing addresses this gap by automatically generating unexpected, malformed, or boundary-case inputs~\cite{miller1990empirical}.

In particular, grammar-based fuzzing generates structured inputs from formal specifications, enabling principled and comprehensible exploration of a program's input space. Unlike random fuzzing, grammar-based fuzzing produces syntactically meaningful inputs, making the relationship between input structure and program behavior visible.

Despite its widespread use in industry~\cite{manes2019art}, fuzzing still receives limited attention in software testing education.
Possible reasons include technical barriers, lack of classroom-friendly tools, and insufficient pedagogical frameworks for introducing fuzzing to students.

This paper describes a structured classroom workshop using \emph{Fandango}~\cite{zamudio2025fandango}, an open-source, language-based fuzzer for Python, to introduce master's students to fuzz testing.
The activity was not designed as a simple tool demonstration, but as a \emph{mindset intervention}: shifting students' attention from ``Does the program produce expected results?'' to ``What kinds of inputs could break this program?''

The study is guided by three research questions (RQs):
\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1] To what extent does the workshop improve students' self-assessed knowledge and confidence in grammar-based fuzzing and robustness-oriented testing?
    \item[RQ2] How do students perceive the workshop in terms of perceived learning, engagement, usability, and cognitive load?
    \item[RQ3] What conceptual and practical difficulties do students encounter, and what do they reveal about the challenges of introducing fuzzing in educational contexts?
\end{description}

The contributions of this paper are threefold.
First, we present a structured, replicable workshop design that moves students from observing a fuzzer to using it on programs of their own.
Second, we report a pilot evaluation showing large self-reported gains in familiarity with grammar-based fuzzing ($p < 0.001$, $r \geq 0.87$), alongside a knowledge item that did not improve.
Third, we identify the practical and conceptual barriers students reported, and release all workshop materials, data, and analysis scripts so that the activity can be reused and evaluated more rigorously elsewhere.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Software Testing Education and the Testing Mindset}

Software testing is frequently underrepresented in programming curricula, with students perceiving it as tedious and secondary to writing code~\cite{garousi2020testing}.
A systematic literature mapping by Garousi et al.~\cite{garousi2020testing} covering over 200 empirical studies found that most testing education research addresses correctness-oriented techniques---unit testing, test case design, and coverage criteria---while robustness, exploratory, and failure-seeking approaches remain comparatively underexplored.
Students trained exclusively on example-based testing develop strong verification skills but may lack the adversarial approach required to detect unusual cases of failure~\cite{ammann2016introduction}.
Novices, in particular, tend to underestimate how boundary inputs and malformed data can cause failures~\cite{mccauley2008debugging}.
This body of work identifies a persistent gap: techniques that explicitly target robustness and exploratory thinking---such as fuzz testing---are rarely embedded in general software engineering courses.

\subsection{Exploratory and Robustness-Oriented Testing}

Exploratory testing~\cite{whittaker2009exploratory} describes a process in which testers actively investigate potential errors instead of confirming functionality.
Robustness testing extends this by explicitly targeting program behavior under abnormal or boundary conditions~\cite{manes2019art}.
Fuzzing operationalizes both: it explores the input space automatically and targets robustness by design.

\subsection{Fuzz Testing: Concepts and Educational Potential}

Fuzz testing was introduced by Miller et al.~\cite{miller1990empirical} and is widely used as an industrial method~\cite{klees2018evaluating}.
Modern fuzzers such as American Fuzzy Lop~\cite{afl2021} and LibFuzzer operate at an industrial scale in real-world applications.
Grammar-based fuzzing~\cite{aschermann2019nautilus, zeller2019fuzzing} generates structured inputs from formal specifications, producing human-readable test cases that illuminate the relationship between input format and program behavior.
This makes grammar-based fuzzing particularly suited for educational settings: students can read, modify, and reason about the generated inputs.
Fandango~\cite{zamudio2025fandango} combines such a grammar with optional Python constraints, which keeps the input specification short enough to read in class.

Despite its importance, fuzzing is rarely addressed in software engineering classes and is usually presented as a security technique instead~\cite{klees2018evaluating}.
This narrow interpretation of fuzzing may hinder its adoption outside security-related classes.
Interactive textbooks such as \emph{The Fuzzing Book}~\cite{zeller2019fuzzing} make these techniques accessible for self-study, but they are not designed as a single, scaffolded classroom session for students without a security background.

\subsection{Hands-on and Experiential Learning in Security and Software Engineering Education}

Effective learning in software engineering requires more than passive instruction; students benefit from active engagement with realistic tasks and immediate feedback~\cite{kolb1984experiential}.
Experiential learning theory~\cite{kolb1984experiential} posits that learning occurs through cycles of concrete experience, reflective observation, and active experimentation---a model that directly informs the workshop design reported in this paper.
The techniques of problem-based learning~\cite{hmelo2004problem} and inquiry-based learning~\cite{prince2004active} provide additional motivation for engaging in structured activities where students are presented with real-world problems that lack predefined solutions.

Moreover, hands-on laboratory activities have also proven effective in security and software engineering education, where tool-supported exercises that expose students to realistic failure modes and adversarial conditions support the development of both technical skills and security-aware reasoning~\cite{du2011seed}.

Prior work has thus extensively examined software testing education, active learning, and fuzzing as a professional technique. However, less attention has been paid to how grammar-based fuzzing can be translated into a short, scaffolded, and empirically evaluated classroom activity for general software engineering students. This study addresses this gap by presenting and evaluating a four-phase workshop that introduces grammar-based fuzzing as a practical vehicle for exploratory and robustness-oriented testing thinking.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Participants and Context}

Participants were 22 master's students enrolled in a software testing course at the Universidade da Beira Interior, Portugal.
Most (21/22) were from Computer Science or Software Engineering programs with intermediate programming experience.
Most had little or no prior exposure to fuzz testing: 18 of the 21 respondents rated their familiarity as 1 or 2 on a 5-point scale.
Participation in the research evaluation was voluntary, anonymous, and conducted under informed consent.

\subsection{Workshop Design}

The workshop lasted approximately 100~minutes and followed a four-phase pedagogical structure, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_fandango_workflow.pdf}
\caption{Workshop workflow: four progressive phases from conceptual introduction to open-ended extension.}
\label{fig:workflow}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Phase 1 --- Conceptual Introduction (20~min)}
The instructor presented the limitations of example-based testing using concrete examples, introduced the concept of fuzz testing, explained the difference between random and grammar-based fuzzing, and demonstrated the Fandango grammar syntax.

\subsubsection{Phase 2 --- Live Demonstration (15~min)}
The instructor ran a live fuzzing session using a deliberately buggy Python calculator program and a corresponding Fandango grammar file (\texttt{.fan}).
Students observed crashes in real time as the fuzzer generated inputs such as \texttt{DIV 7 0}.
The instructor then analyzed a crash trace with the class, identifying the root cause and discussing how a defensive fix would look.

\subsubsection{Phase 3 --- Guided Replication (45~min)}
The instructor distributed the buggy Python code, the Fandango grammar (\texttt{.fan}), and a Markdown guide (\texttt{.md}) with the commands to install and run the fuzzer; setting up a virtual Python environment was explained in class.
Students installed Fandango, ran the provided grammar against the target program, and observed the same crashes the instructor had demonstrated.
The instructor circulated to assist with installation and execution issues.
After reproducing the fuzzing session, students were guided verbally, working on their own machines, to modify the grammar to generate specific input classes (e.g., negative numbers, very large values, empty strings) and observe how each modification affected crash discovery.

\subsubsection{Phase 4 --- Open-Ended Extension (20~min)}
Students chose between two options: (a)~inserting one or more bugs into the calculator and checking whether the fuzzer detected them; or (b)~writing a new Python program of their choice and repeating the whole process, from grammar design to crash analysis.
Such a task forces students to reason about testing from an adversarial perspective: as both developer and tester.
A short group discussion and a post-test questionnaire concluded the workshop.

\subsection{Data Collection}

\subsubsection{Pre-Activity Questionnaire}
Seven Likert self-assessment items (5-point scale) measuring: familiarity with fuzz testing, familiarity with grammar-based fuzzing, command-line interface (CLI) confidence, edge-case understanding, automated test generation understanding, crash interpretation confidence, and understanding of the random-vs.-grammar distinction; and one multiple-choice question (MCQ) on the definition of grammar-based fuzzing.

\subsubsection{Post-Activity Questionnaire}
Same seven Likert items and MCQ; plus post-only constructs: perceived learning (5~items), engagement (3~items), usability (2~items), cognitive load (3~items), and four open-ended questions.

\subsubsection{Anonymous Matching}
Students generated a personal code (last two digits of phone number + birth month + first letter of the mother's first name) used across both questionnaires.
Of the 22 students, 21 completed the pre-questionnaire and 20 the post-questionnaire.
Eighteen codes matched exactly; two more pairs were matched after correcting typing errors in the code (one contained an impossible birth month), giving $N = 20$ pairs, and one student completed only the pre-questionnaire.
Repeating every paired analysis with the 18 exact matches leaves all conclusions unchanged.

\subsection{Data Analysis}

Pre/post Likert differences were compared with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
Zero differences were discarded and tied absolute differences were mid-ranked; exact $p$-values were obtained by enumerating all sign assignments of the ranks, and the effect size is $r = |Z|/\sqrt{N}$, with $Z$ from the tie-corrected normal approximation and $N = 20$ pairs.
The MCQ was compared with an exact McNemar test.
For the post-session constructs we report means, the share of responses of 4 or 5 (``\% agree''), and Cronbach's~$\alpha$.
All analyses used SciPy; the script is in the replication package.
One author grouped the open-ended answers by recurring topic; this was not a formal thematic analysis with independent coders, so we report the groupings as descriptive impressions with counts of students.
Answers written in Portuguese were translated by the authors.

The workshop produced the largest gains on items directly targeting grammar-based fuzzing.
Self-reported familiarity with grammar-based fuzzing increased by $+2.05$ points ($p < 0.001$, $r = 0.888$) and understanding of the random-vs.-grammar distinction by $+2.10$ ($p < 0.001$, $r = 0.885$); all 20 students rated both items higher after the workshop.
General fuzz testing familiarity rose by $+1.75$ ($p < 0.001$, $r = 0.873$).
These gains start from a very low baseline (mean $\approx$ 1.6/5): students had almost no prior exposure to fuzzing.

Regarding robustness-oriented testing competencies, results were more nuanced.
Crash interpretation ($+0.45$, $p = 0.020$, $r = 0.558$) and CLI confidence ($+0.60$, $p = 0.009$, $r = 0.593$) improved significantly, while edge-case understanding and automated test generation showed positive but nonsignificant trends ($p > 0.05$), consistent with these being broader competencies that a single session cannot fully develop.

\textbf{Knowledge item.}
The MCQ, asking which statement best describes grammar-based fuzzing, does not corroborate these self-reported gains.
Before the workshop, 18 of the 20 matched students chose the correct answer; afterwards, 15 did.
Four students switched from the correct answer to a wrong one---three to ``random meaningless bytes''---and one switched the other way (exact McNemar $p = 0.375$).
With 90\% correct at the outset, a single item leaves little room for improvement, but it means the gains above should be read as changes in how students rated themselves, not as demonstrated knowledge.

\subsection{Student Difficulties and What They Reveal}

For RQ3, the 20 students answered four open-ended questions; the counts below are of students.

\textbf{Practical difficulties.}
Installation and environment setup dominated: asked what was most difficult, 11 of the 20 named installing Fandango (\emph{``Some problems installing fandango''}), the Python version (\emph{``Execute the command due to version of python''}), missing prerequisites (\emph{``Having the pre requirements to make the activity''}), compiler setup on Windows, or running commands inside the virtual environment.
Three asked for clearer installation instructions or for the prerequisites to be shared in advance, and two for a room with power outlets.
Environment setup thus remains a nontrivial obstacle for CLI-based educational tools in heterogeneous classrooms.

\textbf{Conceptual difficulties.}
Asked which concept was hardest, five students named fuzzing concepts themselves---grammar-based fuzzing, the fuzzing pipeline, or the difference between random and grammar-based fuzzing (\emph{``Maybe, at the beginning, to understand the difference between random fuzzing and grammar-based fuzzing''})---and two named crash analysis (\emph{``Analysing software crashes''}).
Eight reported no conceptual difficulty.

\textbf{What helped.}
Seven students credited the explanations, slides, or instructor, and five the hands-on practice.
Six asked for more examples, exercises, or time (\emph{``Maybe try to add an extra exercise that try to simulate a more realistic and complex working scenario,''} \emph{``More pratical~[sic] in more hours''}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{The Pedagogical Progression: Observe, Reproduce, Break}

The progression from live demonstration through guided replication to open-ended extension was designed to scaffold the move from passive observation to active, adversarial reasoning.

The live demonstration (Phase~2) showed students a program that appeared to work correctly crash on automatically generated input; this is the experience that sets fuzzing apart from example-based testing.

Guided replication (Phase~3) gave every student the chance to repeat that experience on their own machine.
Distributing the code, grammar, and guide meant students did not have to design a target program or grammar before seeing fuzzing work; installation, however, remained the main obstacle (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The open-ended phase (Phase~4) asked students to reason as both developer and tester: to understand how and why programs fail, and to design inputs that make them fail---the core of the exploratory testing mindset the workshop aims to cultivate.
No log data were collected on this phase, so we cannot report how many students completed it or what faults they found; six students asked for more exercises, examples, or time.

\subsection{Self-Reported Versus Demonstrated Knowledge}

The contrast between the large self-reported gains and the flat knowledge item is the main caveat of this evaluation.
Students clearly left feeling more familiar with grammar-based fuzzing, but the one objective item cannot show whether they also understood it better: it started near ceiling, and the four students who switched to a wrong answer---three of them to ``random meaningless bytes''---may indicate lingering confusion between random and grammar-based fuzzing; five students named fuzzing concepts as the hardest part of the workshop.
With so few changed answers, this cannot be distinguished from careless responding.
Future runs should measure demonstrated skill directly, for example by assessing the grammars students write or the faults their fuzzing finds in Phase~4.

\subsection{Practical and Conceptual Barriers: Implications for Curriculum Design}

The two categories of difficulty highlighted in RQ3 have different implications for curriculum development.
While setup consumed time, dependency management, platform-specific compilation instructions, and virtual environment configurations are real aspects of software engineering work.
Allowing students to encounter and overcome this hurdle under the supervision of the instructor can contribute to professional readiness, something that a ready-made cloud environment cannot provide.
A combination of approaches might prove effective here: sharing installation instructions and prerequisites before the session, as three students suggested, and providing a ready-made environment for those who still cannot install the tools, while leading the class through the installation itself.
The second difficulty, understanding what grammar-based fuzzing does, highlights a more fundamental problem in incorporating fuzzing into educational curricula.
Students accustomed to procedural testing (write assertions, check outputs) must shift to a generative, structural mode of thinking.
This threshold concept warrants dedicated instructional time and may benefit from analogies to other formal notations (e.g., regular expressions, context-free grammars in compilers courses) to activate prior knowledge.

\subsection{Limitations of a Single Session}

The nonsignificant results for edge-case understanding ($p = 0.273$) and automated test generation ($p = 0.072$) suggest that broader robustness-oriented testing skills require more than a single workshop to develop.
A single workshop can introduce the vocabulary, motivate the mindset, and give students an initial opportunity to practice the technique; however, deeper skill development requires integration into the curriculum.
Future workshops should build on this foundation through more complex grammars, multi-session activities, and integration with complementary techniques such as property-based testing and mutation testing.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Internal validity.}
Because the study used a pre/post design without a control group, causal interpretation remains limited.
The students had also taken part in another software-testing teaching session one week earlier, which may have influenced their responses.
The instructor was also one of the researchers, which may have encouraged favorable self-reports.
Reusing the same MCQ item in the pre- and post-questionnaires may also introduce a testing effect.

\textbf{Construct validity.}
Self-reported Likert assessments may not capture actual skill acquisition, and the only objective measure is a single MCQ that was near ceiling before the workshop.
The seven Likert items were designed for this study and have not been validated in prior work.

\textbf{External validity.}
A single class of 22 students at one institution limits generalizability.
Results may differ with students of different backgrounds, different target programs, or different Fandango versions.

\textbf{Reliability.}
Cronbach's $\alpha$ for perceived learning (0.88), engagement (0.86), and usability (0.81) indicates acceptable to good internal consistency.
The cognitive-load items ($\alpha = 0.15$) mix item valence and are therefore reported individually.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

This paper presented a structured classroom workshop that used grammar-based fuzz testing to introduce exploratory and robustness-oriented testing.
Its four phases---concept, demonstration, replication, and extension---scaffold the move from observing a fuzzer to adversarial thinking.

Students reported large gains in familiarity with grammar-based fuzzing ($+2.05$, $p < 0.001$, $r = 0.888$) and in understanding the random-vs.-grammar distinction ($+2.10$, $p < 0.001$, $r = 0.885$), although a single knowledge item did not improve.
They rated the activity as engaging and usable, and installation was the most frequently reported difficulty.

Overall, the findings suggest that grammar-based fuzzing can serve as a practical and engaging classroom pathway into exploratory and robustness-oriented testing, but demonstrating learning will require objective measures and a comparison condition.
Future work should investigate multi-session curricula that build on this foundation, longitudinal retention of the exploratory testing mindset, and the integration of grammar-based fuzzing with mutation testing and property-based testing.
